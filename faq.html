Frequently Asked Questions

Short answers about The Disruptor: what it is, what it costs (nothing), and how it works in teaching. The educators page has the detail.

For students32

What is The Disruptor?

A free, single-player business simulation where you take the role of Chief Innovation Officer at Vélox Dynamics, a Belgian fitness equipment company. You manage R&D projects, alliances, intellectual property, a digital platform, corporate ventures, hiring, standards and more across a three-year tenure. It runs in the browser with no installation and no account.

What is new in Version 2?

The board meets in front of you each year, argues, and the CEO decides where the firm will go. At the end of your first and second years you are appraised: you answer the board’s questions and learn to read the room. You choose how far to bring generative AI into the firm’s innovation work, and live with what it brings. And at the end you are judged twice, by Vélox and by the game, which assesses how you used the tools of corporate entrepreneurship and innovation management.

Is it free?

Yes. No premium tier, no adverts, no registration. It is an open educational project, its content licensed CC BY-NC-SA 4.0 and its software PolyForm Noncommercial 1.0.0, with no fees of any kind. Commercial use requires a separate licence.

What will I be doing?

Running innovation at Vélox Dynamics for three years. Your managers bring you proposals and problems across sixteen systems, and you decide. Each year the board sets a goal and three objectives, and at the end of the year it asks you to explain yourself.

What kind of things happen to me?

More than eight hundred events, and one game shows you only some of them. A patent troll writes from Texas. Hobbyists crack your subscription. An unauthorised side project turns out to be the best idea of the quarter. And the Tour de France closes Vilvoorde for three days, with your deliveries on the wrong side of the barricade.

Who will I be dealing with?

A board that argues in front of you, a CEO who then decides, and managers who each want something different from your budget. Katrien De Wolf, the CEO, opens the yearly meeting with: “Three proposals. I want the case for each, and I want it short.”

What are the yearly objectives?

Each year you are given an income goal and three objectives. They stay in a bar at the top of your screen, and they change with the direction the board chooses. Chasing them while everything else is going on is most of the game.

What is the appraisal?

At the end of your first and second years the board questions you about what you did, and what you did not do. You answer against a clock, wait outside, and come back in for your score. Where the year is close, your answers can make the difference.

What is the Innovation Launchpad?

The Launchpad is where your investments in developing technologies, products, services, and capabilities are translated into innovations that you can prepare for launch into the market or the organisation. All potential innovations come to the Launchpad, and you decide which to launch, which to hold back, and which to kill. This is the authority the board has given you, and it is the area where you are the central decision maker. You feed the Launchpad by using the other systems of the game to seed the pipeline with valuable and useful products, processes, and services. Each launch carries risk, with greater risk and reward associated with more radical innovations. You will need to learn when to kill innovations and when to launch them even when there are mixed signals. Be too bold and you might end up with many costly failures. Be too risk-averse and you lose out on opportunities. The board will set targets for you, but you should not just look for short-term wins. Build the capabilities to generate a rich flywheel of innovations that can power the firm forward. This is the heart of the game, and it is where you will need to act with care but also boldness.

How long does a game take?

A full three-year run takes between 45 minutes and an hour and a half, depending on the player’s experience and engagement. Probation mode covers one year and takes around 30 minutes. You can save the game and restart at any time. If your educator is using the Incubator, you will play in shorter sessions across several weeks rather than in a single sitting.

How hard is it?

It is challenging. Sixteen systems compete for your attention and budget, events change your plans, and outcomes are uncertain. Most players will not get a top score on their first run. Probation gives a gentler start, the Forge is the standard game, and Gauntlet is deliberately punishing. You get better at reading the systems as you play. The game rewards experience and strategic thinking, not memorising a walkthrough.

What systems can I manage?

Sixteen, and they interact. The R&D Portfolio and Innovation Launchpad handle your pipeline from early research through development to market. Innovation Strategy sets the direction. Alliances and University partnerships connect you to external knowledge. Standards let you shape industry rules. The Platform manages your digital ecosystem. IP Management covers patents and trade secrets. Scouting and Crowdsourcing bring in ideas from outside. Venturing and Grant Funding provide alternative routes to innovation. Staff & Hiring builds your team, Organisation shapes how it works (including, on its AI & Innovation tab, how far AI goes in R&D), and Corporate covers the company's heritage and governance, including the Foundation that controls it. Competitors & Markets round out the picture. Each system has its own logic, and decisions in one affect the others. The game does not tell you which to prioritise. That is the job.

Do I need to master all sixteen systems?

No, you do not. The game gives you staff and managers who can handle the detail of individual systems on your behalf. You do not need to negotiate every alliance yourself, review every patent, or manage every collaboration. Your job is to ensure that the different systems are contributing to your goals and objectives, and to decide where to direct your attention when they are not. The players who do well are the ones who focus on the bigger strategic challenges rather than getting lost in the mechanics of each engagement. Trying to control everything yourself is one of the fastest ways to fall behind.

What are the game modes?

The Forge, and three harder versions that the start screen lists as advanced modes and still marks as experimental. The Forge is the standard three-year game with all systems open. Pivot moves Vélox into a new sector (wellness, medical devices, defence or gaming), where it has to build new capabilities and partners. Jugaad halves the budget and makes you innovate with fewer resources. Gauntlet sets punishing targets while core markets erode and competitors close in. Within the Forge, Probation gives you one year to prove yourself, with gentler expectations, and the Incubator unlocks systems one at a time as you go, so you learn each one before the next arrives. Each choice changes what you are up against.

What is Probation?

A one-year variant of the Forge. The board gives you a single year to prove yourself, with gentler targets. At its end comes a Probation Review, and you choose whether to carry on into the full term. The year takes around 30 minutes and covers the core systems without the full three-year commitment. It is a good way to learn the game before taking on a full tenure.

What are the mini-games?

Three arcade games that turn up during a run: Lead User Patrol, Design Thinking Pinball and Wellness Orbit Run. They are fun diversions that reward you with capability boosts, budget and strategic intelligence. You can skip any of them.

What is the Pantheon of Innovation?

A gallery of forty researchers whose work shaped the field you are managing in. You will see their names and ideas come up during play, connecting what you are doing in the simulation to the thinking behind it. Each entry has a biography and key works if you want to read further. Among them are Schumpeter, Freeman, Pavitt, Nelson, Teece, Chesbrough, von Hippel, Christensen, Penrose, Tushman, Henderson, and Eisenhardt.

What mistakes do players make?

Not spending the resources the board gives them. Being unable to kill innovations that are not working, because they have already sunk time and money into them. Being unwilling to take risk on uncertain projects, chasing safe incremental bets instead. Not using the full range of systems available: alliances, grants, scouting, standards all extend what the firm can do, but players ignore them. Chasing short-term income rather than building capabilities and taking long-term bets on new products, categories and services. These are the same patterns you see in real firms, and they show up clearly in your end-of-game report.

What happens if I get fired?

You still get the same ending as a completed tenure: the end screen, your report and the postcard. It records what you did, what went wrong, and where the board lost confidence. Read it, then start again. A dismissal is not wasted time. You made decisions under uncertainty, they had consequences, and the report sets both out clearly enough to learn from. The simulation is a safe place to take risks that would be unthinkable in a real firm, and some of the most useful lessons come from playthroughs that ended early.

Why is every run different?

The game draws from a large pool of events, and a single playthrough only sees a fraction of them. The board’s meeting at the start of each year can set the firm on a different course, and the objectives follow it. The four versions create different strategic problems. Competitors behave differently each time. Two students can play the same mode and have quite different experiences.

Is it worth playing more than once?

Yes, and the second game is the better one. The first goes on working out what the systems do; in the second you have a plan. Each game deals different events and a board that may choose another direction, and there are harder versions when you want them. Keep your report and try to beat your own score.

How does the game end?

With your last day, and then two verdicts: the board's view of your tenure, and The Disruptor's score out of 100, with a level somewhere between Innovation Youngling and Innovation Jedi Master. You keep a report, Your tenure at Vélox, and a postcard.

Can I talk it over with the CEO afterwards?

Yes. Katrien De Wolf will sit down with you for an exit interview about your run. She reads your report first, so download it from the end screen and bring it. No report, no debrief. Open the Executive Debrief →

Can I compare my results with other players?

Yes, in the Forge, and you do not need a class code. Choose Compare at the start and you will see how your years stand against other Forge players. The game gives you a made-up nickname, and nothing about you is recorded. If your educator gave you a class code, enter it and your results join your class's.

Where should I start?

Watch the explainer film first; it takes under five minutes. Then start with Probation in the Forge to learn the systems without the full three-year commitment. If you have time, read the Vélox Dynamics case study first. It sets the context and helps you treat the game as a strategic simulation rather than a resource puzzle. Once you have found your feet, play a full three-year Forge run. The built-in tutorials cover the dashboard and every system.

Can I get help while playing?

Yes. The game has two built-in tutorial modes: a Quick Guide covering eight topics to get you playing in minutes, and a Full Guide walking through every system. There is also a free Player's AI Assistant, hosted on Google's NotebookLM, that answers questions about the game in plain language. It is spoiler-free and will not reveal scoring thresholds or end-of-tenure mechanics.

Why Belgium?

Because it puts you somewhere specific rather than somewhere generic. Vélox Dynamics sits in a real institutional world: European research universities, enterprise-foundation ownership, EU funding instruments and standards bodies. That setting shapes the decisions you face in ways that a placeless company would not.

Why the fitness equipment industry?

Because everyone understands what a fitness equipment company makes, so you can focus on the management decisions rather than learning unfamiliar technology. But the industry is broader than it looks. Vélox sells to consumers and to businesses, runs a connected platform alongside the hardware, and reaches from physical products through data and AI to medical rehabilitation. That range means you face the full spread of innovation management problems in a single firm.

What is the Foundation, and why does it matter?

Vélox Dynamics is listed on Euronext Brussels, but the Van Marcke Foundation for Health and Movement controls it through a dual-class share structure, holding 78% of the voting rights. The Foundation has a mission around health, movement science, and wellness, and the game tracks how well your decisions as CIO align with it across three pillars: environmental, social, and governance. Your choices affect the Purpose score: forming the right alliances can strengthen it, ignoring a supply chain scandal can damage it, and the board will let you know if you are drifting. The commercial side of the business still needs revenue, launches, and market share. Managing both at once is the tension at the heart of the game.

Are you collecting my data?

Only an anonymous count of visits. The game runs entirely in your browser. There is no account, no sign-in and nothing to submit, no cookies are set, and nothing you enter or decide is sent anywhere. The game does use Umami, a privacy-focused analytics tool, to see how people use it: which pages are viewed, approximate location, browser and device type, and how long a session lasts. Your IP address is used only to work out the location and is not stored. Game state lives in memory during the session and is discarded when you close the tab.

Can I suggest features or report bugs?

Yes. Email ammon.salter@wbs.ac.uk or use the Issues tab on the GitHub repository. Players' ideas have shaped every version of the game.

What if I see the game being misused?

The game is free for education and research, and the CC BY-NC-SA 4.0 licence is there to keep it that way. If you come across anyone putting it behind a paywall, selling access, using it in paid consulting or corporate training without a licence, or passing off the materials as their own, please let us know at ammon.salter@wbs.ac.uk. Reports help protect the project for everyone who uses it.

For educators21

What do students need?

A modern web browser. The game sets no cookies, holds no student data, and runs entirely client-side. Students open a link and play.

What does it teach?

Innovation management and corporate entrepreneurship together, through running the innovation function of an established firm. Around twenty-five concepts from the research literature are built into its systems, among them absorptive capacity, organisational ambidexterity, appropriability and value capture, dynamic capabilities, open innovation, path dependence, platform strategy and disruptive innovation. Students meet the ideas in context as they play. The in-game Pantheon of Innovation introduces the relevant scholar at each decision point, connecting the simulation directly to the field. The Educator's Companion maps each system to the underlying theory.

Does it replace my teaching?

No. It complements it. The simulation runs alongside your teaching, giving students an experiential context for the concepts they encounter in your course. Your role is to orchestrate: choosing the mode, framing the start, and leading the debrief.

What courses and levels does it suit?

Innovation management, corporate entrepreneurship, technology strategy, R&D management and related subjects, at undergraduate, postgraduate, MBA, executive-education and doctoral level. The versions let you match difficulty to the cohort: Probation or Incubator for first-timers, Forge for the standard experience, Pivot or Jugaad for specialist topics, Gauntlet for a serious challenge.

Do I need to train my students to play?

No. You post a link. The game has built-in tutorials, help throughout, and the Player's AI Assistant for anyone who wants more. Students can replay as often as they like. Some play once, others go back several times chasing a better score. How much further you go is up to you: the Educator's Pack is there if you want to tie it into your course with session plans, debrief questions and assessment ideas.

Do I need to master the game to use it?

No. Many colleagues have used the game with their students without ever playing it as a game themselves. The game teaches concepts and ideas that most educators in this field understand well already. The Educator's Pack was created to lower the challenges of adoption, and the Educator's AI Assistant can answer questions before class and in real time as they come up. Some of your students will end up knowing the game far better than you. Gamers will look for hacks to gain advantage, just as entrepreneurs do. That is part of the learning.

What is the Incubator?

A teaching variant of the Forge designed to control how students encounter the game's sixteen systems. Students begin with two playable systems (the R&D Portfolio and the Innovation Launchpad) and open others progressively. Students open the systems themselves, so it works whether you set the pace by telling them which to open or let them explore on their own. The board does not end the appointment over a poor record, so students can experiment with little risk of an early game over. It maps directly to a syllabus: if you are teaching alliances this week, unlock alliances; next week, unlock IP strategy. Students play between sessions and bring their experience to class, and each unlock introduces a new layer of complexity. By the end of a module, students are managing all sixteen systems and can see how the decisions connect.

How do I use the Incubator in a course?

The Incubator opens the game’s systems a few at a time, so that play can follow your syllabus. Each week you tell students which system to open, they play outside class, and the session opens with what happened: what they tried in alliances, how their IP choices changed things, what trade-offs they met. The teaching guide in the Educator’s Pack has an example sequence.

Can I use it for assessment?

Through reflection rather than the score. The game scores each player at the end, but that is feedback, not a grade: outcomes carry a large element of chance, so the score alone is a poor measure of learning. What works well is a reflective essay. Every run produces a PDF report the student keeps, and they write about the decisions they made, why, and what they would do differently. The Educator's Pack includes assignment ideas built around this approach. Because every run is different and the report records what each student actually did, work built on it (a reflective essay, a journal kept while playing, a short presentation of the portfolio's post-mortem) has to match the student's own decisions. That makes it harder to hand to a chatbot than a general essay.

How do class competitions work?

You make a class on the Competitive Play page and give your students the code. When a student starts a Forge game they choose Compare and enter the code, or Skip. The game plays the same either way. Results are sent at the end of each year. At the end the student sees where each year stands, and you see the whole class on the page, with a download if you want it. Students without a class code can still compare themselves with other Forge players.

Is it hard to set up?

No. Making a class takes a minute: a label you will recognise and the students' level. There is no account, name, e-mail or password, for you or for them. You keep one class link, which is the only way in to the results.

What personal data is captured?

None. Each student is given a nickname made up by the game. No name is recorded, nothing a student types, and no time of day, and the database keeps no IP address. What is recorded is the result of each Forge year: income against the goal, objectives met, the board's appraisal and score, what was used, time played, how the tenure ended and the game's own assessment. Nicknames and class codes are removed after 12 months. If you ask students which nickname is theirs, you link results to people, and what you download is then yours to look after.

How does it connect to innovation management research?

The game's systems are designed around concepts from the innovation management and corporate entrepreneurship research literature. The in-game Pantheon of Innovation is a gallery of forty scholars from the field, including Freeman, Schumpeter, Pavitt, Nelson, Teece, Chesbrough, von Hippel, Christensen, Penrose, Tushman, Cohen, Levinthal, Henderson, Eisenhardt, and many others. Each has a biography, key works, and contextual advice that appears during play. The Educator's Companion maps each of the sixteen systems to the relevant research, so you can connect what happened in the simulation to the concepts in your syllabus.

Is it flexible?

Very. It runs from a thirty-minute Probation session to a full-semester module, and from first-year undergraduates to executives, with four versions, Probation and the Incubator's progressive unlock. With no install and no account, it drops into a lecture, a workshop, homework or an online class without setup.

Is there an educator pack?

Yes, and it is free on request from ammon.salter@wbs.ac.uk. It includes the Vélox case study, the Educator's Companion, a teaching guide, a fact sheet, two slide decks, a student onboarding sheet, the Player's Companion, a players' manual, learning objectives, an exam question bank, an assignment bank with rubrics, and two AI assistants (one for educators, one for students). The educators page lists everything in the pack. The Educator's Companion is for educators only: please do not share it with students, or post it on the web or on a learning management system.

Is the game still being developed?

Yes. The current version is Version 2, which adds the board in person, the year-end appraisal, generative AI and a new ending. Since its launch on 26 April 2026, the game has been accessed by almost two thousand people in seventy-seven countries and at more than fifty universities. Development is continuous, with bug fixes, balance adjustments and new features driven by playtesting feedback. The game is open source on GitHub, and contributions are welcome.

If it is so good, why is it free?

Because it was built by an academic, not a company, and giving it away is part of the point. Credit comes through citations rather than sales. The game is free for education and research; commercial users pay for a licence, which helps keep it that way. There is no premium version behind the free one. What you play is the whole game.

How does it compare with other innovation management simulations?

Commercial simulations are typically licensed per student, need accounts and setup, and often cover a narrower range of topics. The Disruptor is free and open source, runs in the browser with no setup and no student accounts, covers sixteen interconnected systems across the full breadth of innovation management and corporate entrepreneurship, and comes with a free Educator's Pack. Because the licence is open, you can adapt the game to your course rather than fitting your course around the game.

Who built it?

The Disruptor was created by Ammon Salter, Professor of Technology and Innovation Management at Warwick Business School, University of Warwick. His research covers open and distributed models of innovation, R&D decision-making, innovation and social networks, AI and new ventures, university-industry collaboration, and innovation policy. He is co-author of The Management of Technological Innovation (Oxford University Press, with Mark Dodgson and David Gann) and former co-editor of Research Policy. He has worked with companies, governments, and other organisations on the management of innovation, and teaches the subject at all levels. The game is his attempt to translate research findings into play, so that students can experience the challenges of managing innovation at an established firm rather than just reading about them. He built it himself, with help from colleagues and students, through AI-assisted development, because he is also a lifelong gamer who wanted to bring the feel of commercial games into an educational setting. The Disruptor sits alongside Build, Bin, Boost and the Slingshot as complementary tools for teaching innovation management and corporate entrepreneurship. It is an open, not-for-profit educational project: its content is licensed CC BY-NC-SA 4.0 and its software PolyForm Noncommercial 1.0.0.
